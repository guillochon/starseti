Here's a self-contained summary you can paste into another session.

**The idea.** The "Cepheid galactic internet" was proposed by John Learned, Rolf-Peter Kudritzki, Sandip Pakvasa and Anthony Zee in 2008 (arXiv:0809.0339), not by Fred Adams. An advanced civilization could use Cepheid variables as all-call beacons because they are visible across the Local Group and out to the Virgo cluster, and any technological society monitors them closely as distance indicators. The mechanism is to "tickle" the star with an energetic beam — they suggested neutrinos — near the end of a pulsation cycle so the next expansion triggers slightly early, encoding information in a sequence of small phase shifts. The observable is short-timescale, non-random structure in the O−C (observed-minus-computed) timing residuals of the pulsation.

**The one search.** Hippke, Learned, Zee et al. (2015, ApJ 798, 42) tested this on Kepler data. The RRc star KIC 5520878 showed period-sequence autocorrelation at prime-number lags (p = 99.8%), which briefly looked like a candidate but turned out to be the natural beating of two simultaneous pulsation modes with a period ratio near √2 (companion paper arXiv:1407.3442). It was a SETI null result, with the recommendation that the search be automated as a by-product of routine time-series pipelines. Since then the idea has sat in the SETI literature as a taxonomy example (Wright 2018; Hippke's "Benchmarking information carriers"; Lacki 2015; Chennamangalam et al. 2015 transposed it to pulsars). The 2008 paper has about nine citations, none after 2020, and no new dedicated search had been done.

**The data now available.** TESS (2018–) gives 2-minute or 20-second cadence, ~20 s timing precision per pulsation cycle, on bright stars across multiple sectors and years. ASAS-SN gives all-sky, roughly nightly coverage since 2012–2014 in V and 2018 in g, saturating near g ≈ 11 and useful to ~15.5; it has per-camera zero-point offsets. ZTF covers Dec > −28° at 2–3 night cadence since 2018, ~10 mmag photometry, saturating near g ≈ 12.5–13 and reaching ~19; each object ID is one field/quadrant/filter, so a star appears under several IDs. Gaia DR3 has epoch photometry for ~15,000 Cepheids and 270,000 RR Lyraes but only ~40–70 transits each; DR4 (not before December 2026) will roughly double that and cover all sources. Gaia DR3 also gives a clean starting catalog: 15,021 Cepheids, of which ~5,250 are Galactic.

**Two analysis regimes.** With dense data (TESS), fit a Fourier template and measure a time shift for every individual cycle. With sparse data (ASAS-SN, ZTF, Gaia), individual cycles can't be timed; instead convert each epoch's flux residual to an implied timing offset via the template slope and average in blocks, and — more powerfully — regress squared residuals on squared template slope, whose slope coefficient is the variance of cycle-to-cycle phase jitter. That jitter test recovers a modulation signal that block averaging would erase. It requires the pulsation period to be refined to ~10⁻⁶ d first, otherwise phase drift over a multi-year baseline masquerades as jitter.

**Failure modes, in rough order of frequency.** (1) Template-fit artifacts: whole-cycle time-shift fits are sensitive to where cycle boundaries are cut and to template richness; a spurious secondary period at exactly 4× (or 2×) the pulsation period across stars of very different periods was the tell-tale of this. (2) Light-curve shape variation (amplitude or chi-square changes cycle to cycle) leaking into "timing". (3) Instrumental systematics: TESS sector normalization seams, orbit/sector periods (13.7 / 27.4 d), and momentum-dump intervals (2.5–7 d in early sectors); daily, lunar and annual aliases for ground-based data. (4) Red noise, which trivially defeats a white-noise shuffle null. (5) Real astrophysics: multi-mode pulsation, period doubling, the Blazhko effect, light-travel-time sinusoids from binary companions, and secular period change.

**What a credible candidate would need.** Structure that survives moving the cycle boundaries and enriching the template; agreement between the template O−C and an independent timing method (crossing time on the steepest branch of the rising light curve); no correlation between |O−C| and residual chi-square or per-cycle amplitude; significance against a block-bootstrap null that preserves short-range correlation; the same structure independently present in at least two data segments; and no coherent secondary period that matches known aliases or astrophysical mechanisms. Anything with an isolated autocorrelation spike at a specific lag, rather than smoothly decaying correlation, is the pattern that would resemble a code.

**Where the effort stands.** A first TESS pass over Gaia-selected Cepheids flagged 27 stars for autocorrelated O−C; after vetting, all 27 were attributable to the failure modes above, distributed the way a null sample should be. The layered design going forward is: wide, sparse surveys (ASAS-SN, ZTF, eventually Gaia DR4) to rank a few thousand pulsators by phase-coherence and jitter statistics, then TESS per-cycle timing as the follow-up instrument for whatever they flag — which is the automated, by-product search the 2015 paper called for and nobody had built.
